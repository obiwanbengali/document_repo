Candidate Family Discovery

Discovering candidate Bayesian families

"If federation were chosen, what candidate Bayesian families might exist?"

Instead of one large Bayesian model covering all of NHS Pathways, a federated approach could use several smaller models, each focused on a related area of clinical reasoning. Smaller models are easier to change, test and reason about. The difficulty is deciding where the lines between them should fall.

Candidate Family Discovery investigates where those boundaries might exist. It looks for groupings that the data itself suggests, using three independent kinds of evidence, and then measures how well each proposed grouping actually holds together.

Nothing here has been decided. Every grouping on this page is a candidate. Whether federation is the right approach at all is a Architecture Feasibility & Comparison question, and whether any individual family makes clinical sense is a question for clinical review.

Executive summary

What senior readers need to know

A short synthesis of the evidence before the detailed analysis.

Section 1

How we looked for families

Three independent kinds of evidence were used. Keeping them separate matters: when methods that share no assumptions arrive at similar groupings, that agreement is itself evidence. If only one method had been used, its output could not be distinguished from its assumptions.

Section 2

Comparing the approaches

Fifteen ways of grouping the estate were tested and measured identically. The two qualities that matter pull against each other: a grouping can be recognisable — matching classifications clinicians already use — or self-contained, meaning its pathways mostly refer to each other rather than outside. Few groupings are both.

Section 3

Candidate family explorer

The candidate set presented here is the one that required both kinds of evidence before keeping a group: pathways had to connect to each other and share clinical features. Select a family to see what it contains and how well it holds together.

Every measure has an explanation attached — select the ⓘ marker beside any label.

Section 4

Where candidate families overlap

Candidate families are not cleanly separated. Some hand assessments to each other frequently; some draw on much the same clinical vocabulary. Four different measures of overlap are available, because they can disagree — two families may exchange many assessments while sharing little vocabulary, or the reverse.

Section 5

Pathways that sit on a boundary

Most pathways sit comfortably inside one candidate family: everything they hand over to, and receive from, is in the same family. Others sit at the edge, and a few connect several families at once. Those are the pathways where the choice of boundary matters most.

Section 6

Content shared across many families

Some questions, symptom discriminators, clinical concepts and outcomes appear in many candidate families at once. If families were built as separate models, this content would have to be either duplicated in each one or held somewhere common.

Section 7

How clearly each pathway belongs to one family

If separate models were used, something would have to decide which model handles an incoming case. This section measures how far that decision can be made from the structure of the data alone.

It does not design that mechanism. How routing would actually work in a running system is a Architecture Feasibility & Comparison question.

Section 8

Where families might be too broad, or too closely related

Some candidate families are large and might naturally divide. Others overlap enough to raise the question of whether they are really one family. Both were tested by measuring what would happen — dividing or combining the families and recomputing the same measures.

No merge or split is proposed. These are before-and-after measurements for review, not recommendations.
