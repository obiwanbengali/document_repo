Estate Structure Investigation

Understanding NHS Pathways

"What does NHS Pathways actually look like?"

It is tempting to picture NHS Pathways as several hundred separate assessment flows, each self-contained. The data says otherwise. Questions are shared between pathways, pathways hand assessments over to one another, and the same outcomes are reached from many different starting points.

What the estate actually forms is a single connected clinical decision structure. That matters for any design decision that follows: a structure with this much sharing and cross-referral behaves very differently from a collection of independent parts.

Estate Structure investigation measures that structure and nothing more. It draws no conclusions about how a model should be built.

Section 2

How large are pathways?

A pathway's size is the number of questions it can ask. This is not the number a patient would be asked: most assessments take a short route through a pathway, and the questions available depend on the answers given. It is the size of the content that has to exist.

Size varies enormously, and that variation is the point: a design sized around the typical pathway would not accommodate the largest ones.

Section 3

How much content is reused?

The same clinical question often appears in many pathways. Rather than each pathway having its own private set of questions, the estate draws on a shared catalogue.

This is worth measuring because it describes how separable the estate is. Heavily shared content has to be accounted for by any design — held once, or repeated wherever it is used. Estate Structure investigation measures the sharing; it does not say which approach is better.

Section 4

Pathways are connected

There are two kinds of navigation in the data. Within a pathway, an answer moves the assessment to another question in the same pathway. Between pathways, an answer can hand the whole assessment over to a different pathway entirely — for example, an assessment that begins with one symptom transferring to the pathway for a more specific problem once that becomes apparent.

It is the second kind that shapes the structure of the estate, and it is far more common than one might expect.

Section 5

How Pathways is already grouped

The data already carries several classification systems: clinical areas, symptom groups, age bands and trauma tags. Any of these could in principle be a starting point for dividing the estate.

Two things complicate that. They overlap — a pathway can belong to several at once — and they were designed for clinical navigation and content management, not for dividing up a computational model. Candidate Family Discovery tests how well each of them actually holds together.
